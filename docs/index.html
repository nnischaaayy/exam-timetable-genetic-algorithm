1 · The problem

Give every exam a slot and a room, without hurting any student

A university has 20 exams, 150 students (each taking 3 to 5 exams), 15 time slots (5 days × 3 periods) and 4 rooms (30, 60, 90 and 120 seats).

Hard rules: never break

① No student has two exams in the same slot.
② Room seats cover the exam’s students.
③ A room hosts one exam per slot.
④ Every exam gets exactly one slot and room.

Soft wishes: keep low

Back-to-back: exams in neighbouring periods of one day.
Same-day load: extra exams for a student on one day.

The catch

Two exams may share a slot only if no student takes both and enough rooms are free. Fixing one clash by moving an exam often creates another.

Why it matters: a single clash means a student physically cannot attend two exams. A timetable must be completely valid, and then as comfortable as possible.
2 · Why it is hard

Too many timetables to try them all

3 × 1023

possible timetables: each of 20 exams can sit in any of 15 slots (1520). At a million timetables per second, checking them all would take about 10 billion years.

Does luck work? Roll random timetables and count the clashes. Each roll is a completely random schedule.

–clashes in the last roll
–fewest clashes in any roll
0timetables rolled

A valid timetable needs 0. Random rolls almost never get there: in our Python test, 9,000 random timetables never produced a valid one.

Why we need a smarter search: brute force and luck both fail, but the problem has structure, since good partial timetables can be combined. That is exactly what a genetic algorithm exploits.
3 · The idea

Let timetables evolve

Nature improves animals by survival of the fittest. We do the same with timetables: a population of 60 candidates improves over 150 generations.

1 · Scorepenalty for every timetable
2 · Selecttournament of 3: best of three random timetables
3 · Cross overchild takes the head of one parent, the tail of the other (p = 0.8)
4 · Mutateeach gene gets a random slot with p = 0.05
5 · Keep the best 2elitism: progress is never lost

Try crossover

A timetable is a list of 20 numbers: gene i is the slot (1 to 15) of exam i. Two parents swap parts at a cut point to make two children.

Why each part: selection pushes towards good timetables while keeping variety; crossover mixes good partial solutions; mutation adds new ideas; elitism guarantees the best answer is never lost, so the best-so-far curve only goes down. Too much mutation (0.10 or more) wrecked results in our tests.
4 · How a timetable is scored

One number decides what survives

Penalty = 1000 × clashes + 1000 × room failures + 10 × back-to-back + 2 × load
0

Why those weights: the huge weight on hard rules means one clash outweighs any comfort gain, so the GA first finds a valid timetable and only then polishes comfort. A separate validator, written independently with plain loops, re-checks the final answer.

7 · Is it any good?

Far better than luck, competitive with other methods

Mean final penalty over 10 seeds with about 9,000 evaluations each (lower is better, log scale), measured in Python.

Honest reading: the GA beats random search by a huge margin and beats annealing and hill climbing at this budget, but a simple greedy method is even better, and seeding the GA with it gives the best mean. With four times more evaluations, simulated annealing (475) overtakes the GA (519). The GA is competitive, not magic.
8 · Proof it is correct

We check the answer independently

Independent validator

Re-checks every hard rule with plain loops over students, without the shortcut the fitness uses. The program stops with an error if any hard rule is broken.

10 unit tests

Fitness equals validator on 100 random timetables; room allocator never double-books; GA best-so-far never worsens; same seed gives the same result.

Reproducible

Fixed random seeds for data (7) and runs (0 to 9), plus held-out seeds (100 to 109) that were never used to choose settings.
